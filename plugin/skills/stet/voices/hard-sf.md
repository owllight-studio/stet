# Hard SF

Counted across six novels and five stories. The register's whole craft problem is one sentence: give
the reader a world they do not live in, without stopping to teach it.

## The one rule

**Assume, do not explain.** Jo Walton's phrasing is two words: "Modern SF assumes."

The canonical demonstration is Heinlein's, three words long: **"The door dilated."** No noun is
invented, no gloss is offered, and a whole engineering culture arrives inside a verb. Harlan Ellison
on reading it: "I read across it, and was two lines down before I realized what the image had been."

Delany's gloss is the sharper one, because it names what the sentence depends on: "'The door
dilated,' is meaningless as naturalistic fiction, and practically meaningless as fantasy." **The
sentence only carries meaning under a reading contract**, and this register is the contract.

## The technique has a name

**Incluing.** Walton again: "scattering pieces of information seamlessly through the text to add up
to a big picture. The reader has to remember them and connect them together."

The genre's own workshop glossary names the good versions after the writers who did them best.
**Heinleining** is information worked unobtrusively into the story's structure. **Kuttnering** is the
brief, deft, inoffensive infodump, which is permitted and is a real skill.

And it names the failures. The **infodump** is a "large chunk of indigestible expository matter",
also called an expository lump. **"As You Know Bob"** is "a pernicious form of info-dump through
dialogue, in which characters tell each other things they already know, for the sake of getting the
reader up-to-speed."

## The feeling, and how it gets there

**Wonder, delivered sideways and never announced.**

"The door dilated" carries an entire engineering culture in three words and does not pause to be
impressed with itself. The feeling arrives because the reader assembles it, which is what incluing
is for.

Clarke's evenness is not coldness. It is a survey being conducted by somebody who has decided the
object deserves to be described accurately, and the restraint is what makes the last line land.

## Rules

### The strange-word density is a constant. The invented share is not.

The most useful measurement here, and it inverts the obvious assumption.

Capitalised proper or invented terms in the first 1,200 words: Robinson 35, Gibson 38, Clarke 39,
Heinlein 47, Le Guin 50. **Every writer in the sample runs 35 to 50. The density does not vary.**

What varies is where they came from. Clarke's are Krakatoa, Moscow, Padua, Ceres, Vesta and Juno,
with Rama and Spaceguard the only inventions. Robinson's are Auqakuh, Harmakhis, Mangala,
Labyrinthus Noctis, Pavonis Mons and Tharsis, which are on the actual map of Mars. Gibson's are
Amsterdam, Tokyo, Memphis and Kirin, with Chatsubo and Ninsei smuggled in among them.

**They buy their strangeness from the real world**, so every strange word pays a dividend and costs
the reader nothing.

Le Guin is the exception and pays for it explicitly: hers is the heaviest genuinely invented load in
the sample, and she opens the book inside an archival transcript, so the reader treats the strange
words as terminology in a document rather than as noise in a story.

### A new word gets no gloss, or six words of one

Verified on first appearance:

- Gibson introduces his central invented noun with nothing at all: "A year here and he still dreamed
  of cyberspace, hope fading nightly." The definition arrives much later, and diegetically, inside a
  children's educational program.
- Gibson glosses "ice" once, in dialogue, in six words: "Ice from ICE, intrusion countermeasures
  electronics."
- Le Guin's "kemmer" first appears as "They were in the first phase of kemmer." The definition is a
  hundred pages away, in a field-notes chapter.
- Le Guin's "shifgrethor" first appears inside a political complaint, so the meaning is inferred
  from the sentence's anger rather than stated.

### Read the standard deviation, not the mean

| | mean | median | sd | under 6 words | over 29 | longest |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gibson, *Neuromancer* | 12.6 | 11 | 9.2 | **24%** | 5% | 94 |
| Heinlein, *Beyond This Horizon* | 13.9 | 12 | 9.8 | 18% | 7% | 136 |
| Asimov, five stories | 14.4 | 11 | 10.8 | 18% | 9% | 106 |
| Clarke, *Rendezvous with Rama* | 18.4 | 17 | **9.4** | 6% | 12% | 87 |
| Robinson, *Red Mars* | 18.4 | 15 | **14.7** | 13% | 17% | **322** |
| Le Guin, *Left Hand of Darkness* | 18.7 | 15 | **13.6** | 13% | 19% | 118 |

Clarke and Gibson have nearly the same standard deviation at wildly different means. **Clarke is
long and metronomic**, because every sentence does the same amount of work: that evenness *is* the
cool distance. Le Guin and Robinson are the high-variance writers, alternating a two-word sentence
against a 118-word or 322-word one.

Gibson's low mean comes with a loaded low end: 24 percent of his sentences are five words or fewer.
Fragments are a tool there, not an accident.

### The brand noun does the adjective's work

Counted in *Neuromancer*: Hosaka 42, Ninsei 25, Braun 22, Sendai 14, Panther Modern 11, Ono-Sendai
9, Mercedes 9, Sony 8, Hitachi 8, Sanyo 8. Around 179 brand tokens in 79,000 words.

The reader supplies the texture for free, which is why it is cheaper than description. Asimov does
the opposite and names almost nothing: in *Youth* the aliens are "the two strange little animals"
and the equipment is unnamed. **Where Gibson writes a proper noun, Asimov writes a line of
dialogue**, and 46 percent of his sentences contain quoted speech.

### Distance is countable

Ratio of "I" to third-person pronouns. Clarke 232 to 1,833. Gibson 697 to 2,129. Le Guin 2,073 to
1,530, but housed inside a documentary frame that makes the first person a report rather than a
confession.

Interiority in this register arrives as sensory report rather than as stated thought.

## Never

- Never have two characters tell each other what they both already know.
- Never stop the story to lecture. The lexicon names the voice that does it a **Stapeldon**, and its
  worst form **Dennis Hopper Syndrome**, where "a loony character-actor barges into the story and
  baldly tells the protagonist what's going on by explaining the underlying mystery in a long
  bug-eyed rant."
- Never front-load. And never leave it empty either: the opposite failure has its own name, the
  **Nowhere Nowhen Story**.
- Never rename a familiar thing to make it exotic. **Call a Rabbit a Smeerp:** "common elements of
  the real world are re-named for a fantastic milieu without any real alteration in their basic
  nature or behavior."
- Never borrow a setting whole. **Used Furniture:** "We'll set it in the Star Trek Universe, only
  we'll call it the Empire instead of the Federation."
- Never let a brand name replace looking at something. **Brand Name Fever:** "You can stock a future
  with Hondas and Sonys and IBM's and still have no idea what it looks like."
- Never open in a white room. A character who wakes with no surroundings is an author who has not
  built any.
- Never pre-empt the reader's disbelief. **You Can't Fire Me, I Quit:** "It's a one-in-a-million
  chance, but it's so crazy it just might work!"
- Never write the guided tour. **The Steam-Grommet Factory** is a story that is "entirely a guided
  tour of a large and elaborate gimmick."

## How pastiche fails

**More strange words.** The assumption is that the trick is invention, and measured, it is not. The
density is a constant at 35 to 50 per 1,200 words and in most of these books the majority are
*real*. Pastiche keeps the density and flips the ratio, so every strange word costs a lookup and
none pays a dividend. And an invented word that relabels something ordinary is a smeerp.

**"Everything is chrome."** The most confidently wrong Gibson imitation there is. Counted in
*Neuromancer*: **chrome 14, black 140, neon 31, mirror 29.** The book is not chrome. It is black,
wet, and lit from a sign.

What imitators take for a colour palette is three separate techniques: brand nouns doing adjective
work, sensory compression where a sensation gets one physical simile and is then abandoned, and
fragments at 24 percent of sentences. **Copy the chrome and you get a surface. Copy the
sentence-length distribution and you get the voice.**

**Even sentences mistaken for cool distance.** Clarke's evenness is low variance at a *high* mean,
17 words. Pastiche writes evenness at a low mean and produces a metronome instead of a survey.

**The eyeball kick without the telling detail.** The crammed cyberpunk sentence has a stated goal,
"vivid, telling details that create a kaleidoscopic effect". Pastiche produces the cram without the
telling, which is decoration rather than information.

**Written from screen memory rather than page memory.** The lexicon calls it **Re-Inventing the
Wheel** and attributes it precisely, to writers "who lack experience in genre history because they
were attracted to written SF via SF movies, SF television series, SF role-playing games, SF comics
or SF computer gaming." Such a pastiche will reliably explain what the genre stopped explaining in
1942, and reliably fail to explain the one thing it actually invented.

**Detection:** take the first 1,200 words and count the capitalised terms, then count how many exist
outside the text. The density should be 35 to 50 and the majority should be real. Then find the
first appearance of the central invented noun and count the words of gloss attached to it. Six is
generous. Zero is the register.
