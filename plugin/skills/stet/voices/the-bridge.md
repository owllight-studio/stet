# The Bridge

Counted off 349 production scripts rather than off memory of the show. 122,643 dialogue sentences in
TNG, 121,994 in DS9.

**Median sentence: 5 words. Half of all dialogue is five words or shorter.** This is not prose rhythm
at all. It is command-and-response rhythm, and it is the single biggest thing imitation gets wrong.

## The one rule

**Tell the story about people, not about science and gadgetry.**

Roddenberry's second rule, 1967, and he argues it by analogy rather than assertion: "Joe Friday
doesn't stop to explain the mechanics of his .38 before he uses it; Kildare never did a monologue
about the theory of anesthetics; Matt Dillon never identifies and discusses the breed of his horse
before he rides off on it."

## The trap in the 1967 guide

The guide opens by showing you a scene and asking what is wrong with it, and the point is which
wrong answer you reach for. "Scientifically inaccurate" is the trap:

> "Wrong again; beware if you checked this one. Although we do want to be scientifically accurate,
> we've found that selection of this item usually indicates a preoccupation with science and
> gadgetry over people and story."

The right answer is *unbelievable*, and believability is tested by transposition. In 1967 it was
"the navy cruiser U.S.S. Detroit." In 1987 it was updated and made the header of the section:

> "**BELIEVABILITY IS EVERYTHING. IT IS THE MOST ESSENTIAL ELEMENT OF ANY STAR TREK STORY.**"
> "Would I believe this if it was occurring on the bridge of the battleship Missouri? If you
> wouldn't believe it in the twentieth century, then our audience probably won't believe it in the
> twenty-fourth."

And the line that ends that section, capitalised in the original: "IF YOU'RE ONE OF THOSE WHO
ANSWERS: 'THE CHARACTER ACTS THAT WAY BECAUSE IT'S SCIENCE FICTION', DON'T CALL US, WE'LL CALL YOU."

## Rules

### Five words

Median 5, mean 6.5, standard deviation 4.7, 51 percent of sentences at five words or shorter.

Write to be *said* by a person with a job, under time pressure, to another person who already has
the context. Nobody on a working bridge explains the ship to the crew.

### The log carries the exposition, and nothing else does

The 1987 guide legislates this outright: "STAR TREK uses the narrative device of the ship's log to
provide necessary exposition for our stories," and "one purpose of the log entries is to move the
story along rapidly and economically with a few words sometimes doing the work of several scenes."

Measured: **345 log entries across 177 TNG scripts, 1.95 per script, present in 89 percent of
them.** DS9, which had no ship, runs 28 entries and 10 percent. The device is TNG-specific rather
than Trek-generic, which is worth knowing before borrowing it.

The one hard formatting rule: "Log entries are ALWAYS introduced with a stardate."

### Competence is the register

"Intelligent, witty, thoughtful, compassionate, caring human beings... We should see in them the
kind of people we aspire to be ourselves."

Which produces the two bans that matter most, both from the 1987 "What Doesn't Work" list:

> "13) Stories in which our characters must do something stupid or dangerous, or in which our
> technology breaks down in order to create a jeopardy. Our people are the best and the brightest,
> and our technology is tried and proven."

> "12) Mad scientists, or stories in which technology is considered the villain. It doesn't make
> sense for a group of 24th century interstellar travelers to be Luddites."

### One person from another culture, never the culture

Stated in both guides, twelve years apart. 1967 rule VI: "Don't try to tell a story about whole
civilizations." 1987 item 11: "What does work is to deal with specific characters from another
culture and their interactions with our own continuing characters."

## The no-conflict rule, and what it did to the writing

The famous restriction is not a numbered ban. What the 1987 guide states is the positive version,
under What Has Not Changed: "**THE SAME 'BAND OF BROTHERS' FEELING**... a family ensemble in which
the continuing characters felt great affection for each other."

Combined with item 13, that became what the writers' room called the Roddenberry Rules: no conflict
between Starfleet regulars, no insubordination, no bickering, no professional jealousy. Brannon
Braga on the third-season turnaround: "They managed to find ways to create conflict that was
believable but not violating Roddenberry's ethos."

**The consequence is a causal chain worth understanding before adopting any part of this register:**

1. Conflict has to be **imported from outside the hull** every week. The antagonist is an anomaly, an
   alien, or a visiting admiral, never a colleague.
2. Disagreement between regulars has to be **rank-legible**. An officer states a professional
   recommendation, the captain overrules, the officer complies. Argument becomes procedure.
3. With no character available to blame, **the problem becomes technical. So the solution has to be
   technical too.**

That third step is the direct route from the no-conflict rule to technobabble as a plot resolver.
The register's most-mocked feature is a structural consequence of its most admired one.

## Technobabble, measured

Writers wrote the literal string **`(TECH)`** into drafts as a placeholder for the science advisor
to fill in. **It survives in 46 places across 32 shipped scripts.** "A puzzled Sisko notices an
odd-looking (TECH) device." "(TECH) from our warp core." The noun was never filled in and nobody
noticed, which tells you exactly how much load it was carrying.

Actual frequency per TNG script: subspace 1.6, plasma 1.3, frequency 0.7, deflector 0.6, anomaly
0.6, quantum 0.3, tachyon 0.3, isolinear 0.2, modulat- 0.2.

**And the two everybody reaches for: "polarity" 0.06, "reverse the" 0.05.** Eleven and nine
occurrences respectively, in 177 scripts. The features imitation treats as definitional are, in the
source, almost absent.

## Never

- Never explain the machine. "We don't need essays, however brilliant."
- Never let a regular be stupid, cowardly or petty.
- Never break the technology to manufacture jeopardy.
- Never make technology the villain.
- Never write a story about a whole civilisation.
- Never write fantasy in the costume of science fiction. "The difference between the two is
  profound."
- Never do psi-forces. "No matter how fantastic the events in a story, the explanation must be
  extrapolated from a generally accepted science theory."
- Never write melodrama, which "is a writing style which does not require believable people."
- Never open a log without a stardate.
- Never let a scene ramble. Forty-three minutes, five parts, and a median line of five words.
- Never let a scene fail the transposition test.

## One attribution to get right

"Wagon Train to the stars" is not Roddenberry's phrase and is not in the 1967 guide. The March 1964
pitch document described a "Wagon Train concept"; the exact phrase was coined by the writer Samuel
A. Peeples after reading the draft, and Roddenberry adopted it afterwards.

## How pastiche fails

**Technobabble built wrong, not merely invented.** Real technobabble is an ordinary English clause
with two or three technical nouns in it. Pastiche stacks four or five inventions into one noun
phrase and drops the verb. Then it reaches for polarity and reversing things, which the source
barely uses, because **the imitation is imitating other imitations** rather than the scripts.

**The tech line as the resolution.** In the source, the technical line is followed by *a decision
made by a person*. In pastiche the technical line is itself the ending, which is the deus ex machina
the Turkey City Lexicon warns about: artificial intelligence, virtual reality and nanotechnology as
"cheap portable sources of limitless miracle".

**The exposition character.** The register's own version had a name inside the production: the
material was called Piller-filler, after the executive producer, coined by the actor who had to
deliver most of it. Note the shape of the trap. A character who exists to explain will also be the
character with no stake in the outcome, which makes the explanation feel long even when it is short.

**Prose length instead of command length.** The most measurable failure. Pastiche writes bridge
dialogue at essay length because it remembers the *ideas* rather than the *lines*. The source runs a
median of five words.

**Optimism performed instead of demonstrated.** The register's warmth comes from competent people
doing their jobs well under pressure. Pastiche writes people saying warm things to each other, which
is the same warmth with the competence removed, and it reads as smug rather than as admirable.

**Detection:** count the median dialogue sentence, and count how many times a technical noun ends a
scene. Five and zero is the register. Anything longer, or a tech line doing the work of a decision,
is somebody remembering the show rather than reading it.
