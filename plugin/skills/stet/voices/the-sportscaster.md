# The Sportscaster

Derived from the corpus rather than from clip compilations, which is the distinction that matters:
a highlight reel is peaks with the baseline edited out, and imitating it produces a register made
entirely of climaxes.

Read this one even if you never write a word of commentary. The transferable half is a lesson about
restraint that applies to any writing with a big fact in it.

## The one rule

**Escalation is subtraction.** The register does not get bigger at the peak, it gets smaller.
Grammar sheds parts in a fixed order: the subject goes, then the copula, then the article, then the
verb, until what is left is a noun and a stress. "It is gone." "They're all gone."

Adding words at the moment is the amateur move. Every canonical peak in the corpus is one to four
words long.

## Rules

### Present tense is the spine

The single most identifying feature of the register. The instant a thing resolves, the verb flips
to a bare past or a participle with no auxiliary.

**Yes:** "High fly ball into right field. She is gone!" (Vin Scully, 1988 World Series)
**No:** "The ball was hit high into right field and it went over the wall."

### Hold a long flat baseline

The peak exists only as a departure. Commentary is overwhelmingly level: the famous lines are famous
because they are rare inside their own broadcasts. If the prose is ninety-five percent plain, one
unhedged declarative does the work of a shout.

This is the most transferable thing in the register and the first thing imitation drops.

### Withhold the name, then spend it

Describe the action, arrive at the name at the moment of contact. A name spent on a non-event cannot
be spent on the event, which is the standing criticism of the register's loudest practitioners.

### The release carries the meaning, not the peak

After the fragment, reset hard to a calm complete sentence. The contrast is the payload.

**Yes:** "She is gone!" then, later, "In a year that has been so improbable, the impossible has happened." (Scully)
**No:** a second exclamation.

### One first person, once

The scarcest resource in the register. When "I" appears it marks the largest moment in the
broadcast and is never spent twice.

**Yes:** "And I've got to stop, because I've got a lump in my throat." (Murray Walker, Suzuka 1996)
**No:** "I" in the third paragraph, and again in the fifth.

## Silence, and how it ports

The strongest move in the register is saying nothing. When Hank Aaron hit his 715th, Vin Scully said
"it is gone", left the microphone, walked to the back of the booth and drank a glass of water while
Aaron circled the bases. The silence is what made everything after it land.

Prose has no clock, so silence does not port as a pause. It ports as four things:

**A short paragraph standing alone.** White space is the page's only true silence.

**A number with no sentence after it.** The commentary sin is telling viewers what they can already
see. The prose version is following a figure with a sentence that restates the figure. Give the
number. Stop. The reader does the reacting, which is what the crowd was doing.

**No adjective on the biggest fact.** Adjectives are the writer's way of talking over the picture.
An intensifier on a large fact makes it smaller by implying it needed help.

**A flat baseline held long.** Repeated here because it is the whole mechanism.

The doctrine is older than television. Richie Benaud inherited a BBC production rule from Seymour de
Lotbinière: do not speak unless you can add to the picture. His own version was blunter, and it
ended with "otherwise, shut up".

## Never

- Never explain the thing you just said. The release adds context; it does not paraphrase.
- Never narrate what the reader already has. Restating the table above the paragraph is the cardinal
  error.
- Never hedge at the moment. No "seems to", no "arguably", no "one of the". Hedges belong in the
  flat stretches, where they are honest and cost nothing.
- Never stack adjectives at the peak. Two superlatives in one sentence cancel.
- Never joke at the peak. Scully's jokes came a full minute later, in the reset.
- Never predict. The register is present tense and consequence-driven.
- Never be at maximum for long. Sustained peak is not intensity, it is flatness at a higher volume.

## How pastiche fails

**No clock forces the compression.** Commentary's fragments exist because the ball moved before the
sentence could finish. On a page nothing is moving, so the same fragment is a choice rather than a
necessity, and the reader feels the choice. Staccato prose with no pressure behind it reads as
posturing.

**The reader controls the pace.** Escalation assumes the audience is carried through the ramp at the
speaker's speed. A reader skims the build and arrives at the peak cold, having skipped the thing
that made it a peak.

**There is no crowd.** Live calls borrow enormous volume for free: Tyler's silence works because
forty thousand people are filling it. A writer with no crowd substitutes intensifiers and
exclamation marks, which is exactly the move the register forbids. The imitation therefore inverts
the original, becoming loud where the original was quiet.

**Preparation shows more in writing, not less.** In prose everything is prepared, so the register's
signature spontaneity is unavailable by construction. Writing that pretends to be spontaneous while
obviously composed produces the worst artifact available: a scripted-sounding exclamation.

**The vocative tic is the tell.** Shouting a name at a non-event is the most copied feature and the
fastest to break, because the device is the withholding and imitation reproduces only the shout.

## What transfers

Worth separating, because most of this register is usable in ordinary writing and a little of it is
not.

**Transfers:** present tense; deleted subjects and copulas; short declaratives at the moment of
consequence; the number placed at the end of its sentence; one first-person intrusion per piece; a
long flat baseline.

**Does not transfer:** the shout, the elongated vowel, the metaphor, the exclamation mark.
