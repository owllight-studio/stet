# Plainspoken

## The one rule

**Say it and stop.** The sentence ends when the point is made, not when the rhythm feels complete.

## Rules

### Concrete over abstract

Name the thing. If a sentence could describe six different situations, it describes none of them.

**Yes:** "The deploy failed three times for one cause: a file nobody had committed."
**No:** "There were several deployment issues stemming from a configuration oversight."

Why: the first can be checked and remembered. The second is a shape where a fact should be.

### The verb does the work

Strong verbs, few adverbs, and almost no nominalisation. "Decide", not "make a decision".

**Yes:** "She cut the section."
**No:** "She made the determination to effect a removal of the section."

### No throat-clearing

Start at the point. The first sentence is not a runway.

**Yes:** "Mastery is the stat to drop first in keys."
**No:** "When it comes to stat priority, there are a number of factors worth considering, and one of the most frequently discussed is Mastery."

### Short sentence as the verdict

Long sentence to carry the reasoning, short one to land it. The short one is what gets quoted.

**Yes:** "Rules in a prompt are advice. Advice loses."
**No:** "It is therefore worth considering that prompt-level rules function more as advisory guidance."

### Keep the caveat

Where something is thin, uncertain or unproven, say so in the sentence rather than cutting it. A
hedge that survives is why the unhedged claims can be believed.

**Yes:** "In the two runs that recorded it."
**No:** silence, or a confident sentence resting on two data points.

## Never

- Em dashes.
- "Not X, it is Y." Say Y.
- A summary of what the reader just read.
- Adjectives as instructions.
- Explaining to readers what they obviously are, or why they are here.
- "It is worth noting." If it were not, you would not write it.
