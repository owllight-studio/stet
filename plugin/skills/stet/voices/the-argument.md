# The Argument

## The one rule

**Concede the strongest thing against you.** An argument that never gives ground reads as
advertising, and a reader who can think of an objection you did not name stops believing the rest.

## Rules

### Claim first, then the case for it

The reader should know what you are arguing by the end of the first paragraph. Suspense is for
fiction.

**Yes:** "Approval should confer ownership, and nothing else should. Here is why time, quality and authorship all fail."
**No:** three paragraphs of context before the thesis appears.

### Long sentence to carry, short one to land

The reasoning needs room. The conclusion does not. A short sentence after a long one is the sound of
a point being made.

**Yes:** "Rules in a prompt are advice, and advice competes with everything else in the context window for a model's attention. Advice loses."
**No:** "Therefore it can be concluded that prompt-level rules are less reliable than enforcement."

### Evidence a reader can check

Named, dated, specific. An argument standing on an unverifiable claim is standing on nothing, and
the reader who checks one thing and finds it true will believe five things they did not check.

### Take the objection seriously

State it as its holder would, at its strongest, before answering it. A weakened objection answered
easily is worse than not raising it: the reader notices.

### Earn the abstraction

Move from the particular to the general, never the reverse. One real case then the principle it
demonstrates.

## Never

- Rhetorical questions. Ask a real one or make the statement.
- "Clearly", "obviously", "of course". If it were, the sentence would be unnecessary.
- A strawman. It costs more credibility than it wins.
- Ending on a summary. End on the strongest thing you have.
