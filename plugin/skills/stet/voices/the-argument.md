# The Argument

Measured across five essays: Orwell, Didion, Pullum and two of Graham's, 757 sentences in total.

## The one rule

**Make claims as strong as they can be made without becoming false.** Graham's dial, and the whole
register in a sentence. Weaker than that and the essay is not worth reading. Stronger and it is not
worth trusting.

## The feeling, and how it gets there

**The pleasure of watching somebody think, and change their mind on the page.**

The essay register is the most openly emotional thing in the core group, and its emotion is
intellectual: the specific feeling of a mind moving.

Didion's authority comes from having said "I" and meant it, calling writing "an aggressive, even a
hostile act". The unearned "we" is the pastiche failure precisely because it borrows that warmth
without the exposure.

The concession rule is emotional in the same way. A concession before your strongest claim is
generosity, and the reader feels it as such. The same concession after it is retreat, and they feel
that too.

## Rules

### Variance carries the argument

Measured standard deviations run 0.6 to 0.8 of the mean. Didion's is the widest recorded here at
16.6 against a median of 17, which means she is not writing long, she is **writing wide**.

Between a quarter and 38 percent of sentences in every measured essay are ten words or fewer, and
those are the claims. The long ones are the working-out. The register buys the right to a 90-word
sentence by spending most of its sentences under 20.

### Keep the qualifier, and pick the right one

Against the standard advice to strip hedges: *"'I think x' is a weaker statement than simply 'x.'
Which is exactly why you need 'I think.'"*

And qualifiers are not one dial. There are dozens, and they express different things: how broadly
something applies, how you know it, how certain you are, how it could be falsified. Choosing the
wrong one is a failure of precision, not of confidence.

### Concede before the strongest claim, never after

Placement is the whole technique. **A concession before your best argument is armour. The same
concession after it is retreat.**

Pullum concedes that the style advice he is attacking is harmless, and that his target "often wrote
beautifully", *before* demonstrating that three of four cited passives are not passives. The
concessions are what make the demolition land.

Pre-empt the misreadings a smart, well-intentioned reader would make. Do not pre-empt bad-faith
ones: "you don't want to spoil your house by putting bars on the windows."

### The thesis may move, and that movement is the essay

Three placements are all attested. Refuse it outright, because an essay "doesn't begin with a
statement, but with a question". State it as a bare claim and then qualify it downward as the piece
proceeds. Or plant it in the first line and never restate it.

What none of them do is state a thesis and then defend it unchanged. The five-paragraph form, where
the topic sentence is chosen in advance and the paragraphs are "the blows you strike in the
conflict", is the thing this register exists in opposition to.

### Move downward, from the general to the particular

Didion's transitions are almost all downward, and the particular is always physical: a picture, a
sentence, a woman in an airport. The general is then inferred rather than restated.

The two failure directions are worth naming. Particular to general, upward, produces the moral: "and
isn't that true of all of us." General to general produces the essay that never touches anything.

### Brevity is the diction of command

Graham's line, and it comes with a cost he states plainly: elegance and curtness are two names for
the same thing. Curtness is what you spend to get authority, and it is not always the right purchase.

## How to end

Five moves. **Summarising is not one of them.**

**Drop to an image and stop.** Orwell ends twelve thousand words of argument on a physical act with a
small object in it: a worn-out phrase going into the dustbin.

**Return to the opening and escalate it.** Pullum opens "I won't be celebrating" and closes by saying
exactly how he will not be spending April. The reader recognises the frame, and the escalation is
proof the argument worked.

**Disclose what you learned writing it.** Graham ends an essay about essays with the admission that
he did not realise its conclusion when he started, which is itself evidence for the claim.

**State the consequence.** Not what you argued, what follows from it.

**Compress the thesis into something shorter and stronger than its first statement.** "It's hard to
be right without sounding right." Eight words, phrasing that has not appeared before, and a stronger
claim than the opening rather than a recap of it.

Two hard constraints. It must use material already in the piece: a new fact in the last paragraph
means the structure failed. And it must be shorter than the paragraph before it.

**The test: delete your last paragraph.** If the essay reads as finished, it was a summary and it
should stay deleted.

## Never

- Never restate the argument at the end. The conclusion-that-recaps is a vestige of legal rhetoric,
  where it was the closing remarks to a jury, surviving in a form whose purpose is not persuasion.
- Never peter out. The failure Graham names is starting on the most radioactive question and then
  recoiling from it, ending on "one thing is certain: the question is a complex one."
- Never ask a rhetorical question you do not then answer with a fact.
- Never say "clearly" or "obviously". If it were, the sentence would be unnecessary.
- Never build a strawman. It costs more credibility than it wins.
- Never digress without paying for it. Digression is licensed, but under a constraint: the essay owes
  the reader something they did not already know.

## How pastiche fails

The signature is **claim-altitude with no evidence under it**: sentences that sound like conclusions
when nothing has been concluded.

**The aphorism with no referent.** The register earns compression at the end because the middle did
the work. Pastiche opens at that altitude. The test: swap the nouns for different nouns. If the
sentence is still true, it was never about the original nouns.

**The unearned "we".** Didion's authority comes from having said "I" and meant it, calling writing
"an aggressive, even a hostile act". Pastiche jumps to "we" to borrow the warmth without the
exposure.

**The colon that promises depth**, where the second half restates the first half in more solemn
words.

**"Not X but Y", saturated.** A genuine move in this register and the most stealable one. Three in a
page and the register has flipped from thinking to intoning.

**The escalating tricolon.** Three parallel clauses, each longer, ending on an abstract noun. It is a
speech cadence, and it signals that a conclusion is arriving whether or not one is.

**The summary ending in disguise.** Pastiche knows not to summarise, so it summarises *lyrically*:
the last paragraph recaps with an image bolted on. Same test: delete it. If nothing is lost, it was
a summary wearing a metaphor.

**Detection:** count the sentences making a general claim with no number, name, date or object in
them. In the measured essays that ratio is low and clusters at section ends. Uniform across the
text, the piece is intoning rather than arguing. Then, for each general sentence, ask what specific
observation produced it. If there is none, it is decoration.

## One warning about using this beside Plainspoken

**The two pastiches are cured by opposite moves.** Plain-style imitation is fixed by *adding*
variance: a longer sentence, a subordinate clause, a qualifier. Essay imitation is fixed by
*removing* altitude: cut the aphorism and put back the thing that was observed.

A single instruction to write better, applied to both, makes each one worse.
