# Epic Fantasy

Counted across roughly 640,000 words of the canon plus all of *The Lord of the Rings*, against
modern controls. Almost every received idea about this register is measurably backwards.

## The one rule

**A plain language is the noblest of all.** Le Guin's line, and the measurements agree with her.

The instinct is to reach up: longer sentences, rarer words, older grammar. Every one of those is
wrong, and the numbers are not close.

## Tolkien is the plainest writer in the room

Mean words per sentence:

| | mean | median | 10 words or fewer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Morris, *Well at the World's End* | 49.6 | 40 | |
| Dunsany, *Elfland's Daughter* | 30.4 | 27 | |
| Eddison, *The Worm Ouroboros* | 28.2 | 23 | |
| Malory, *Morte d'Arthur* | 26.4 | 22 | |
| Fitzgerald, *Gatsby* (control) | 19.1 | 16 | |
| **Tolkien, LOTR** | **17.3** | **14** | **36.5%** |

**He is shorter than the modern control.** Shorter than Austen, Stevenson, Wells, and shorter than a
1915 thriller. Variance is high, so the texture is short-short-short-long rather than uniformly
stately.

He said so himself, in a letter he drafted and never sent: "a real archaic English is far more terse
than modern." Imitators write long. That is the first and largest mechanical error.

And the vocabulary goes the same way. Latinate suffixes per thousand tokens: Gatsby 24.6, Eddison
14.8, Dunsany 14.6, Malory 9.4, Morris 8.0, **Tolkien 5.9**. Percentage of tokens drawn from the
top-1,000 word list: Malory 87.7, Morris 86.7, **Gatsby 72.7**.

**The archaic register uses shorter words and a smaller, more repetitive vocabulary than naturalistic
prose.** It gets its elevation from rhythm, word order and repetition instead.

## The feeling, and how it gets there

**Grandeur, and Le Guin is the authority on why it fails.**

Lewis's rule, "never an adjective that tells the reader how to feel", is the opposite of a ban on
feeling. Those words are "only like saying to your readers, 'Please will you do my job for me.'"

Le Guin's charge against the flat style is explicitly that it has no feeling in it: "the rocks, the
wind, the trees are not there, are not felt; the scenery is cardboard, or plastic." Her word for it
is journalism, and her diagnosis of the writers who mock their own material is loss of nerve, not
excess of it. Their heroes "keep turning aside to look out of the book at you and whisper, 'See,
we're really just plain folks.'"

The feeling is the whole point. Her line for it: "In fantasy there is nothing but the writer's
vision of the world."

## Rules

### The constructions everybody imitates are the ones he does not use

Rates per 10,000 words:

| | Tolkien | Morris | Malory | Austen |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| "Then said he" (verb-second) | **0.08** | 3.65 | 2.95 | 0.00 |
| Inverted tag "said he" | **0.50** | 1.77 | 6.97 | **7.03** |
| *thou / thee / thy* | **1.68** | 70.38 | 63.19 | 0.00 |
| Fronted phrase + inversion | **1.49** | 0.24 | 0.00 | 0.00 |
| Sentence-initial "For" (= because) | **5.82** | 0.00 | 1.89 | 0.47 |

**"Then said he" occurs once in 477,518 words.** It is not a Tolkien construction. It is the
imitators' construction, and it is the loudest single tell of pastiche.

**The inverted speech tag is rarer in Tolkien than in Jane Austen.** He writes `said <Name>` 2,522
times and `he said` 893 times, against 24 "said he".

**Eighty *thou*-forms in the entire novel.** Morris runs it at about 114 times that rate. Anything
reaching for "thou" is imitating Morris, not Tolkien. And note where they are not: "The King of the
Golden Hall", the chapter a correspondent called terrible for its archaism, contains two.

### The inversion he does use is not archaic at all

"Behind him came Gimli." "Before them rose Bree-hill." This is his highest rate in the set, and it
is ordinary modern presentative word order.

Tolkien flagged the confusion himself, about his line "Helms too they chose": "Some (wrongly) class
it as 'inversion', since normal order is 'They also chose helmets'." He calls it fronting. He is
right.

### Sentence-initial "For" is the genuine signature

454 sentences open with it, 1.64 percent of the novel, the highest rate in the comparison set.
Higher than Malory, and infinitely higher than Morris, who never does it. The others prefer it
mid-clause. He puts it at the head.

### Prefer the Germanic word. Do not ban the Latinate one.

Germanic share of content-word tokens: Morris 84.4, **Tolkien 76.6**, a *Beowulf* translation 75.2,
Malory 68.9, Wells 61.0, Austen 50.2.

He is more Germanic than a verse translation of *Beowulf*. **But measured by vocabulary types rather
than tokens he is 48.1 percent Romance, a higher Romance share than Stevenson or Malory.** The
preference is a frequency effect, not a lexicon effect.

He criticised Morris's purism for exactly this, faulting his "dead and unintelligible 'leeds'".

### The lexical stratum says which culture is speaking

The best finding here, because it usually gets asserted thematically and instead falls out of a word
count. Germanic share is flat across the six books, 75.8 to 77.9, and does not track archaism at
all. At chapter level it runs 65.7 to 81.1.

- **Least Germanic chapter in the novel: "A Long-expected Party", 65.7.** The Shire opening.
  Over-represented words: envelope, invitations, presents, dinner, favourite, pavilion, special,
  cousin, party, money. That is the domestic-social register of the English novel.
- **Saruman, 72.6:** peace, voice, command, power, doubt. Abstract political nouns.
- **Gondor under siege, 70.8:** retreat, garrison, engines, companies, citadel, captain, assault,
  defence. Military-administrative Latin.
- **Most Germanic:** "The Taming of Sméagol" 81.1, "Lothlórien" 80.8, "The King of the Golden Hall"
  80.7, "Treebeard" 80.4.

**Rohan is Germanic. Gondor is Rome. The Shire is Trollope. Saruman is a politician.**

In a secondary world the lexical stratum is the only signal available, because there is no borrowed
history to carry it. That makes vocabulary part of the worldbuilding rather than part of the
decoration.

### Register tracks the thread, not the page number

Archaic markers per 10,000 words: Book I 6.00, Book II 17.77, Book III 19.05, Book IV 8.30, **Book V
41.25**, Book VI 12.98.

**The style does not simply climb as the book proceeds.** Books IV and VI sit near Shire level. The
peak is the Rohan and Gondor thread at nearly seven times Book I, and it drops by two thirds
afterwards. Chapter extremes run "The Passing of the Grey Company" at 70.1 against **"The Uruk-Hai"
at 0.0** and "The Scouring of the Shire" at 1.8.

The span inside one novel, roughly 70 to 0, is far wider than the gap between this novel and a plain
modern thriller.

And the inversion worth keeping: **the most modern idiom in the book belongs to the Orcs**, who
speak in army dispatches and bureaucratic grumbling.

### One consciousness per chapter, and the order is a separate pass

Martin's discipline is architectural rather than lexical. 344 chapters across 1,736,054 words, 31
POV characters, and the per-POV counts **sum exactly to the published chapter totals in all five
books**. That partition only closes if every chapter belongs to exactly one POV with no remainder,
which is the cleanest available proof of the rule.

He drafts several successive chapters from one character, then rearranges them "to optimize
character intercutting, chronology, and suspense". The POV block is the drafting unit and the
interleave is a separate editorial pass.

**And the reordering is where the effect lives.** Intervals between significant deaths in *reading*
order are geometric, meaning memoryless and unpredictable. The same deaths in *story chronology* fit
a power law, meaning clustered. The unpredictability is manufactured by the reordering pass, not by
the events.

### Lewis's five rules, which are the closest thing the genre has to a house style guide

The two that carry the most weight:

**Never an adjective that tells the reader how to feel.** Those words "are only like saying to your
readers, 'Please will you do my job for me.'"

**Never an abstract noun where a concrete one will do.** "If you mean 'More people died' don't say
'Mortality rose.'"

And the one about the ceiling: "Don't say 'infinitely' when you mean 'very': otherwise you'll have
no word left when you want to talk about something *really* infinite."

## Le Guin's argument, which is the essay on this problem

"From Elfland to Poughkeepsie", 1973. Her frame: bad fantasy writers are "building six-lane highways
and trailer parks with drive-in movies, so that the tourists can feel at home just as if they were
back in Poughkeepsie". Her thesis is one line: "the point about Elfland is that you are not at home
there."

**The demonstration is the argument.** She quotes a dialogue between two lords of a fantasy kingdom,
then reprints it having **changed four words**, turning them into a senator and a lobbyist in
Washington. Her verdict: "If it was fantasy, I couldn't have pulled the dirty trick on it by
changing four words. You can't clip Pegasus' wings that easily, not if he has wings." She then runs
the test on Eddison, Kenneth Morris and Tolkien, and it cannot be done to any of them.

**The failure she names is not archaism. It is journalism.**

> "To what then is it appropriate? To journalism. It is journalistic prose."
> "In journalism, the suppression of the author's personality and sensibility is deliberate."
> "A language intended to express the immediate and the trivial is applied to the remote and the
> elemental."

And the distinction that matters most, because the failing style also looks plain and direct: **"It
is a fake plainness. It is not really simple, but flat. It is not really clear, but inexact. Its
directness is specious."**

Measured, her ear was right. Latinate-suffix density in her four sample passages: the one she
condemns scores 47 per 1,000. The Eddison she praises scores 0. The Tolkien she praises scores 0,
with 81 percent of its words four letters or shorter and a mean sentence of 8.3.

**Her diagnostic:** "Speech expresses character. It does so whether the speaker or the author knows
it or not." Then, of a lord saying *I could have told you that at Cardosa*: "I think I know
something about that man. He is the kind who says, 'I told you so.'" Followed by the sentence that
is the whole essay in miniature:

> **"Nobody who says, 'I told you so' has ever been, or will ever be, a hero."**

**Why style is not optional in this genre specifically**, and the paragraph any guide should open
with:

> "In fantasy there is nothing but the writer's vision of the world."
> "There is no comfortable matrix of the commonplace to substitute for the imagination."
> "There is only a construct built in a void, with every joint and seam and nail exposed."
> "The style, of course, is the book. If you remove the cake, all you have left is a recipe."

She treats failed archaism as the beginner's trap rather than the main disease: "The archaic manner
is indeed a perfect distancer, but you have to do it perfectly. It's a high wire: one slip spoils
all." Her catalogue of slips is specific. Fake second-person verbs, "Whithersoever thou goest there
also I goest. Fake feeling: fake grammar." Subjunctives where "all the was's turn into were's, and
leap out at the reader snarling". The case error: "Him shall die?" sounds "like Tonto talking to the
Lone Ranger". Then the vocabulary, and the closing test: **"the infallible touchstone of the
seventh-rate: Ichor."**

She also rejects tonal code-switching, and her diagnosis is nerve rather than taste: characters who
"keep turning aside to look out of the book at you and whisper, 'See, we're really just plain
folks.'"

And she notes archaism is not required at all. Of Jack Vance: "It is an achieved style. And it
contains no archaisms at all." Also: "You don't have to know how to use the subjunctive in order to
be a wizard."

## Never

- **Never "Then said he."** Once in 477,518 words.
- **Never *thou* as decoration.** Eighty tokens in the whole novel.
- **Never *ichor*.** Once in 172,566 words of Eddison; zero across Dunsany, Morris and Malory. Zero
  in roughly 640,000 words of the four masters combined.
- **Never *tush, pish, zounds, marry*.** Zero in LOTR. Tolkien named these four as the definition of
  the vice and then never used one.
- **Never *eldritch, tenebrous, smaragd, chalcedony*.** Five occurrences across 640,000 words, all
  in Eddison.
- **Never long sentences as a proxy for grandeur.** Median 14 words; 36.5 percent at ten or fewer.
- **Never a Latinate abstract noun where the register is heroic.** The condemned passages score 47
  Latinate suffixes per 1,000; the praised ones score 0.
- **Never head-hop.**
- **Never an adjective that tells the reader how to feel.**
- **Never vague scenery.** Le Guin's specific charge against the flat style: "the rocks, the wind,
  the trees are not there, are not felt; the scenery is cardboard, or plastic."

## How pastiche fails

**It has a proper name, and it is a century older than fantasy publishing.** *Wardour Street
English*, coined in 1888 attacking William Morris: "not literary English of any date; this is
Wardour-Street Early English, a perfectly modern article with a sham appearance of the real antique
about it." Wardour Street was the London trade in reproduction furniture sold as genuine antiques.
Fowler gave it currency in 1926 and deprecated a word list: anent, aught, ere, erstwhile, haply,
maugre, oft, perchance, thither, to wit, varlet, withal, wot.

**And here is the mechanism in a single number.** Density of Fowler's own deprecated list: **Morris
2.19 per 1,000. Genuine Malory 0.86.**

**The pastiche is two and a half times more archaic than the real medieval text it imitates.**

That is the whole failure. Imitation overshoots, because the imitator is copying the *marked*
features and the original writer was not marking anything.

Le Guin's word list and Fowler's overlap on *ere*, *perchance* and *thither*, written 85 years apart
with no contact. Two critics independently flagging the same words is about as close to replication
as literary criticism gets.

**The "ye olde" problem.** "Ye olde" is a typographic accident. The article was written with a
thorn, and Caxton's imported Belgian and Dutch fonts had no thorn, so printers substituted a
visually similar `y`. It was never pronounced "yee". A surface artefact mistaken for the thing
itself, then reproduced with confidence, which is the failure in miniature.

The empirical version: Dunsany, the most imitated stylist in the genre, scores essentially zero on
every archaic surface marker. Zero *ere*, zero *ichor*, zero *mayhap*, zero *perchance*, three
"-eth" verbs. **His distance comes entirely from 34.6 percent of sentences opening with "And"**, and
a 30-word mean. Le Guin saw it in 1973: imitation Dunsany "consisted of anything beyond a lot of
elaborate made-up names, some vague descriptions of gorgeous cities and unmentionable dooms, and a
great many sentences beginning with 'And.'"

**The thesaurus problem.** Covered above and worth repeating because the instinct is so strong: the
register is lexically *poorer* than naturalistic prose. Reaching up the register spends the ceiling
on page one.

**The map-and-appendix problem.** Copying the deliverables instead of the pressure that produced
them. Jane Yolen: "what began in grace and power easily degenerated into a kind of mythic
silliness", with "pastel unicorns, coy talking swords, and a paint-by-number medieval setting".
Diana Wynne Jones's *Tough Guide to Fantasyland* is the whole catalogue as a tourist brochure, and
her entries are diagnostic because they name the *absences*: of industry, "most Tours encounter no
industry at all. Even the EMBROIDERY factories are kept well out of sight." Le Guin got there first:
"a writer may use all the trappings of fantasy without ever actually imagining anything."

**The tonal-coherence problem.** The reader can feel the author not believing it. Note this is the
opposite of a call for solemnity: Clarke and Pratchett are both funny and both succeed, because the
tone is consistent.

## What the successes share

**The register is load-bearing, not decorative.**

Clarke keeps her period surface smooth for a stated structural reason: "otherwise the deliberate
contrast between 'the fields we know' and Faerie becomes much weaker." Tolkien's Latinate stratum
marks Gondor as Rome and the Shire as an English novel, so the vocabulary *is* the worldbuilding.
Martin's POV lock is what makes the brutality land, because you are inside one skull when it
happens. Pratchett's register-levelling only reads as a joke if the high register was legible first,
and his own account of the mechanism is "trip up a cliche or take things absolutely literally."

In every case the style is doing a job nothing else in the book can do, which is Le Guin's point
exactly.

**Detection:** run her four-word test. Change the proper nouns to modern ones and see whether the
passage survives as a memo. Then count *thou*, *ere*, *perchance* and *ichor*: any of them present at
a rate above roughly one per 10,000 words means the writer is imitating the imitators.
