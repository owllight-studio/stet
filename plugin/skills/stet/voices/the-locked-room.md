# The Locked Room

The only genre that published its own rules, which makes it the best documented register there is.
Counted here across five books: *The Mysterious Affair at Styles*, *Poirot Investigates*, *Whose
Body?*, *The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes* and *The Hound of the Baskervilles*.

## The one rule

**The clue is on the page, in plain language, and missed.**

Van Dine states both halves and never reconciles them, which is the craft problem the whole register
exists to solve. Rule 1: "All clues must be plainly stated and described." Rule 15: the solution
should have been "staring him in the face" on a reread.

Everything below is the mechanics of satisfying both at once.

## The measurement that kills the received wisdom

| | mean sentence | median | under 11 words | over 29 | adverbs per 1,000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Christie, *Styles* | 9.9 | **8** | **64%** | 3% | **21.5** |
| Christie, *Poirot Investigates* | 10.6 | 8.5 | 61% | 3% | 18.6 |
| Sayers, *Whose Body?* | 13.3 | 10 | 54% | 9% | 14.7 |
| Doyle, *Adventures* | 14.8 | 12 | 46% | 11% | **12.1** |
| Doyle, *Hound* | 14.7 | 12 | 42% | 10% | 11.3 |

Christie's sentences run a third shorter than Doyle's and she writes three times fewer long ones.
Two thirds of her sentences fit in ten words.

**And she uses nearly twice as many adverbs as he does.** Her prose is plain because the sentences
are short, the clauses few and the words common. Not because it was scrubbed. Anyone imitating
Christie by deleting adverbs is following a rule she did not follow.

One more inversion: `said` per thousand words runs 9.2 in Sayers against 4.4 and 3.8 in Christie.
The most literary writer in the sample uses the plainest tag most often.

## Rules

### Uniform prose is the camouflage

The functional reason for the plainness, and it follows from Van Dine 16, which forbids "long
descriptive passages, no literary dallying with side-issues... no 'atmospheric' preoccupations."

**Every sentence is a potential hiding place, and hiding places have to look alike.** Prose with
varied texture tells the reader where to look. A suddenly vivid sentence is a pointer whether or not
you meant it as one.

### Hide it in a list, and never last

The measured case. In *Styles*, the clue the entire solution turns on appears once, at 36.6 percent
through the book, and its whole first appearance is this:

> "Can you tell me what they were? Sulphonal? Veronal?"
> "No, they were bromide powders."
> "Ah! Thank you, mademoiselle; good morning."

Five words of answer, third in a list of three, closed off by a polite exit line. A list tells the
reader "this is background", and the eye reads lists for shape rather than content.

### Hand the attention off immediately

The sentence after the clue carries a stronger stimulus. In that passage, the next paragraph has
Poirot's eyes going "green like a cat's", so the reader records *Poirot got excited here* and files
the scene under the excitement. The memory is indexed on the wrong thing.

The strongest version attaches the fact to an emotional beat: grief, flirtation, embarrassment, a
joke. The reader keeps the feeling and drops the fact, because they believe the paragraph was about
the feeling.

### Plant once, then go silent

Measured: 36.6 percent to 87.2 percent, a gap of more than half the book. **A clue repeated is a
clue underlined.** Mentioned once and abandoned, it reads as texture.

### Let the reader supply the false inference

State something true whose obvious inference is wrong, and never state the inference yourself.

This is the only technique that survives Van Dine 2, which permits "no wilful tricks or deceptions
other than those played legitimately by the criminal on the detective himself." The author has not
lied. The reader has. Scaled to book length it is the *Roger Ackroyd* device.

### The narrator may be wrong out loud, and may hide nothing

Knox specifies the Watson exactly: he must not conceal "any thoughts which pass through his mind",
and his intelligence sits "slightly, but very slightly, below that of the average reader."

That asymmetry is the entire licence. The detective's conclusions may be hidden because the narrator
does not have them. The narrator's own thoughts may not be. Hastings in *Styles*: "Still, it is
unimportant, and need not be taken into account." Poirot's reply is the book's thesis: **"Everything
must be taken into account."**

First-person density is nearly identical across Hastings and Watson, around 25 to 31 per thousand
words. It is a fixed narrative posture rather than a stylistic choice.

### Build the reveal as a monologue with interruptions

Measured on *Styles*, chapter 13. It runs 4,146 words, 7.3 percent of the book, and 77 percent of
its characters sit inside quotation marks. Longest uninterrupted speech: 237 words. Then 157, 137,
136, 120, 119, 119, 116.

**So the mode is roughly a 120-word block, then a question or an objection, then another block.**
The interruptions are the pacing device. Four thousand unbroken words is unreadable.

The order: assembled audience, statement of method, the evidence enumerated *in order of discovery
rather than importance*, each item re-read against its original scene, the false solution
demolished, the accusation, then the confession. The emotional payoff is last and deliberately
brief.

Note what the enumeration does. "I found: first, a fragment of green material; second, a stain on
the carpet near the window, still damp; thirdly, an empty box of bromide powders." That is the same
list device that hid the clue, run in reverse, now demanding the attention it previously deflected.

### No new evidence in the reveal

Knox operating as a pacing rule rather than a plot one: nothing "which will need a long scientific
explanation at the end." In *Styles* the only new material is a printed pharmacy formula, which is
public knowledge the reader could have looked up.

## Never

- Never conceal a clue the detective has and the narrator saw. The Detection Club oath settles the
  whole argument in one line: "Do you solemnly swear never to conceal a vital clue from the reader?"
- Never solve it by coincidence, accident, unmotivated confession or intuition. The oath bans
  "Divine Revelation, Feminine Intuition, Mumbo-Jumbo, Jiggery-Pokery, Coincidence or the Act of
  God."
- Never introduce new physical evidence in the reveal.
- Never invent a poison, an element or an instrument. Van Dine: limit yourself "toxicologically
  speaking, to the pharmacopoeia."
- Never pin it on a stranger or a walk-on.
- Never let the supernatural resolve anything.
- Never let the detective not detect. "A detective is not a detective unless he detects."
- Never let the clue sentence have a different texture from its neighbours.
- Never underline. "Something about it struck me as odd" converts a clue into an announcement.
- Never repeat the clue before the reveal.
- Never let the Watson conceal a thought. He may misvalue anything and must hide nothing.
- Never write the reveal as one unbroken speech.
- Never mistake plainness for adverb deletion.

## The rules that died, named as dead

Worth knowing which parts of the canon are period furniture, because pastiche imports them whole.

No love interest died with *Gaudy Night*. One culprit died with *Murder on the Orient Express*. One
detective died with the police procedural. Never-an-accident-or-suicide is now a legitimate twist.
The bans on servants and on professional criminals as culprits were class prejudice dressed as
craft, and modern crime inverted both.

**Knox's fifth commandment is racist and is named here only so it is not restated.** It excludes a
people, using a slur, and its charitable reading (that it was a swipe at pulp orientalism) does not
survive its own wording. It has no successor in modern practice. The only usable residue is general:
a character who exists to be exotic is bad writing and a bad clue.

And the two best books in the genre break the rules on purpose. *The Murder of Roger Ackroyd*
violates Knox 1, Knox 9 and Van Dine 4 at once, which is why the argument about whether Christie
cheated is the most useful argument the genre has had. **The rules matter most as a vocabulary for
saying precisely what a book did.**

## How pastiche fails

**The over-clued mystery.** The writer is anxious about fairness and compensates by flagging. The
tell is a stress marker attached to the clue: a character pauses, the narrator notes something was
odd, or the clue gets a paragraph to itself. Christie's is five words, third in a list, followed
instantly by something more interesting. Pastiche gives it a paragraph and a reaction shot.

**The withheld clue.** The rules exist principally to name this, which is why they are worth having.
Withholding a fact the narrator knew is not a twist, it is a broken contract. The modern variant is
the detective who solves it from an offstage database lookup the reader never saw.

**The quirky detective instead of the intelligent one.** Quirk is cheap and deduction is expensive,
so pastiche buys the quirk. The test: delete the eccentricity and see whether any deduction fails.
In Doyle the tics and the method are one faculty, all forms of the same obsessive attention. In
pastiche they are decoration bolted onto ordinary police work.

**Period voice performed at the wrong length.** The measured trap. Writers reaching for the Golden
Age write long, formal, sub-claused sentences, because *old* reads as *elaborate*. Christie's median
is eight words. Prose that sounds ornate is not imitating Christie, it is imitating a memory of
Christie.

**The stale device.** Van Dine's rule 20 lists ten that were already exhausted in 1928: the
cigarette butt, the fake séance, forged fingerprints, the dummy alibi, the dog that does not bark,
the identical twin, the hypodermic, the locked room entered after the police broke in, the
word-association test, the cipher. Pastiche reaches for these because they are what the register
looks like from outside. They are what it looked like from outside a century ago.

**The Watson written as an idiot.** The specification is *slightly* below the reader. Made stupid,
he turns the detective into a bully and makes the reader's own wrong guesses feel unshared. The
device works only when reader and narrator are wrong together.

**Implausibility the prose cannot carry.** Chandler's charge, and it stands: "If the situation is
false, you cannot even accept it as a light novel, for there is no story for the light novel to be
about."

**Detection:** for each clue, measure the distance between plant and payoff, and check what sits in
the sentence immediately after the plant. In *Styles* those are half a book and a stronger stimulus.
If the gap is short, or the next sentence is quiet, the clue has been announced.

**And the failure underneath all of them:** every rule in this register is a rule about the reader's
experience, not about the crime. Pastiche optimises the puzzle. The puzzle was never the product.
Sayers wrote the oath a curse rather than a specification, and the curse is the best thing in it:
"may your pages swarm with misprints and may your sales continually diminish."
