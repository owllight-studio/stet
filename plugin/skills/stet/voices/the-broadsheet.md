# The Broadsheet

Taken from the published style guides rather than from an impression of how news sounds. Reuters is
the purest case: an agency selling copy to outlets of every political stripe, so its rules are
engineered to strip the writer out of the sentence entirely.

The first version of this file recommended "according to" as a neutral attribution. Reuters files it
under loaded words.

## The one rule

**Every claim is sourced, and the sourcing verb is "said".** Not "according to", not "revealed", not
"noted". Reuters: those verbs "all contain an element of judgment by the reporter."

The finding that surprises people is the symmetry. *Alleged*, *claimed* and *conceded* imply
disbelief. *Noted*, *pointed out*, *recalled* and *emphasised* imply endorsement. **Both are
banned.** A sympathetic verb is as much a thumb on the scale as a sceptical one.

## Rules

### Count the words in the first sentence

Reuters gives a hard scale: "If there are more than 25, start to get nervous. If there are more than
30 then get very nervous. By the time you reach 40 it's time to break the sentence in two."

Then count the words before the first indispensable one: "If you go beyond three or four words
before reaching that 'must have' word then stop and rewrite."

### News first, source second, unless it is contentious

Lead on what happened. The exception is precise: when the story is an allegation, when the source is
either a major figure or a weak one, or when the report carries doubt. Then the source leads,
because the reader needs to know who is claiming it before they read the claim.

Broadcast inverts this. The BBC, writing for the ear: "always say who before you say what they said
or did", because "this construction puts impact before information".

### Two adjectives, then rewrite

"If you use more than two adjectives before a single noun then rewrite the sentence." A countable
limit, and colour is supposed to come "from strong verbs and facts, rather than adjectives".

### Days of the week, never yesterday

Wire copy is read at an unknown hour, so relative time is meaningless. "On Tuesday", not "yesterday".
Vagueness is a fault of its own: no "several months ago" or "recently", which "suggest we do not know
when something happened or are too lazy to find out".

### Give the actor

**Yes:** "People make demands. Fires kill people."
**No:** "Demands came. The fire left people dead."

### Never adopt the subject's framing

Reuters on naming: an operation's official name is what one side called it. Write what happened.
Scare quotes and "so-called" are barred for the same reason, since both imply a verdict.

### Numbers, and repeat the surprising ones

Spell out one to nine, figures from ten. Numerals always for ages, percentages, dimensions, and
before million or billion. And a belt-and-braces trick worth stealing: repeat an unexpected figure
in brackets so a transmission error cannot silently change it.

The BBC puts arithmetic in its style guide, which tells you something: "A doubling is an increase of
100 per cent. If something triples in value, it goes up by 200 per cent (not 300)."

## The inverted pyramid, and the honest case against it

It exists for two production facts, neither about readers: an editor closing a page could delete
paragraphs from the bottom without reading them, and a reader who stopped early still had the news.

The origin story about Civil War telegraph wires does not survive the archive. The better-supported
drivers are cost, at roughly a penny a character, and commerce: agencies selling one story to papers
of every party needed copy stripped of opinion. **The register's objectivity and its shape have the
same parent, and that parent is a billing model.**

The strongest argument against it is not that it is boring. It is that **the engineering
justification is gone.** Nobody cuts from the bottom, because there is no bottom. What survives is
the reader-scanning claim, which is weaker and applies to news rather than to argument.

Where a piece must persuade rather than inform, the pyramid fights it, because a conclusion stated
before its evidence is an assertion. The Economist states the opposing goal exactly: an article
should be "a series of paragraphs that follow logically in order and, ideally, will suffer if even
one sentence is cut out."

Those two designs are incompatible, which is why The Economist is **not** in this register despite
being a newspaper. Reuters is anonymous because the institution vouches for the facts. The Economist
is anonymous because the collective vouches for the view.

## Never

- Never put opinion in a news story. Features get no exemption.
- Never leave a claim unattributed. Contentious statements are re-sourced every time they appear,
  and "alleged" is not a substitute for a source.
- Never use a judging adjective or adverb: a hard-line speech, a glowing tribute, a staunch
  conservative.
- Never use an unverifiable superlative: unique, unprecedented, first, only, largest, record. If
  somebody else claims it, attribute the claim to them.
- Never read minds. "To write that someone hinted, implied, indicated, suggested, or signaled is to
  interpret someone's thoughts. This is rarely acceptable."
- Never qualify a denial. No "flatly denied", no "categorically denied", unless it is inside a quote.
- Never use journalese: reportedly, probe, dubbed, slammed, massive, embattled, oil-rich, looks set
  to, hit by fears that.
- Never use euphemism. "Died of cancer", not "passed away after a fight with cancer".
- Never alter a quotation, even to fix grammar.
- Never boast about your own reporting.
- Never begin with a question. "The audience wants to be informed, not take part in a quiz."

## The correction

The most systematised part of the register and the least imitated, and it is worth adopting whole
even by people who never write news.

**Four states, chosen by consequence rather than embarrassment.**

| | Used for |
|---|---|
| **Kill** | totally wrong, or so flawed a correction is impossible |
| **Corrected** | a factual error that changes the meaning or undermines credibility |
| **Refile** | a typo or a misspelled name that changes nothing material |
| **Repeat** | no error at all, redistribution only |

A wrong day of the week is a refile. A wrong direction on a price move is a correction. A dropped
word is a refile, unless the word was "not".

**A correction says what was wrong, in words.** Not "this story has been updated". Name the
paragraph and state the error and the fact.

**It stays visible.** Reuters engineers this: the corrected story is not replaced by the next
update, so the correction cannot be quietly overwritten by a later version of the truth.

**Quiet fixes are forbidden.** AP: no euphemisms such as "recasts", "fixes" or "clarifies", and "a
correction must always be labeled a correction".

**The duty follows the error.** AP asks everyone who used the wrong information to run the
correction too.

**The person who erred does not decide.** The desk does, and even where a reporter may fix at source,
a second pair of eyes is required.

**And the tone is fixed.** AP: mistakes are corrected "fully, quickly and ungrudgingly". That last
adverb is the whole convention. A correction that argues with itself is not a correction.
