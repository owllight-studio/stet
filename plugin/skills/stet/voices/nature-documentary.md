# Nature Documentary

Measured from transcripts rather than memory: 1,175 sentences of classic Attenborough narration and
992 of Planet Earth II, plus Herzog's two films in full.

The first version of this file was written from impression and got the rhythm exactly backwards.

## The one rule

**The awe is the measurement.** Real narration is dense with numbers, 7.8 numerals per thousand
words, because the fact is what produces the feeling. Pastiche reaches for "magnificent". The real
thing reaches for "200 tons".

**Yes:** "Its tongue weighs as much as an elephant." (Blue Planet, 2001)
**No:** "This magnificent creature is truly enormous."

## Rules

### Never say "I"

Zero occurrences across roughly 13,500 words of Attenborough voiceover. This is not modesty, it is a
grammatical rule. The narrator has no body: the animal is the subject of every sentence and the
speaker is a property of the film rather than a character in it.

Emotion is delegated to the animal's situation and never claimed by the speaker.

### Present tense, and short

Present outnumbers past eight to one in the classic corpus and twenty-five to one in the modern.
Past appears almost only in framing, never in describing what is on screen.

Median sentence: 13 words in 2001, 10 by 2016. Across 37 years the register lost about a third of
its sentence length. Most imitation runs roughly twice as long as the thing it imitates, because
aiming for grand and stately produces a 25-word median.

### Locate, then reveal

The signature move. Roughly one sentence in four opens with a phrase closed by a comma before the
main clause arrives: a place, a time, a condition.

**Yes:** "But here a mountain is in gestation." (Planet Earth, 2006)

### Lengths cluster, they do not alternate

**This is where the first draft of this file was wrong**, and it repeated the most common piece of
bad advice about the register. Long sentence then a short one for impact is not what the corpus
does.

Measured: the correlation between one sentence's length and the next is **+0.16**. Lengths run in
streaks. After a sentence of 20 words or more, the next averages 15.5 against an overall mean of
13.9, and is *less* likely than baseline to be short.

The real pattern is several long descriptive sentences together while the sequence builds, then a
run of short ones at the moment of action, then back. **The contrast is between paragraphs, not
between adjacent sentences.** A text that metronomes long-short-long-short reads as manufactured
within four sentences.

### Add, never caption

The narration supplies a fact, a scale, a consequence or a stake. It never describes what the viewer
can already see. In prose, the equivalent sin is a sentence that restates the table above it.

### Hedge the animal, not yourself

Uncertainty attaches to the behaviour: "may", "might", "perhaps". Never to the narrator's
confidence. There is no "it seems to me", no "arguably", no "one might say".

## Three registers, split by pronoun

Not two poles on a dial. Three answers to different questions, and the pronoun is what separates
them.

**The absent witness.** Attenborough, and the Nat Geo house voice. No "I", almost no "you", the
animal is the grammatical subject. This is the unmarked case and the centre of gravity.

**The participant.** Steve Irwin. The narrator is in the shot, the animal is reacting to him, and he
turns to camera. High second person because he is talking to you about his own risk.

**The arguing author.** Herzog, and Jane Goodall much more gently. First person is load-bearing:
"I see only the overwhelming indifference of nature" is a claim about the narrator, offered against
the images. Herzog is not louder than Attenborough. He is quieter, and he asserts a thesis where
Attenborough asserts facts.

Because the split is grammatical, a single stray "I" collapses the first register instantly. Most
failed imitations are absent-witness prose with participant punctuation.

*Unmeasured: no transcript of Irwin's or Goodall's narration was reachable. Their circulating quotes
come from interviews and books rather than voiceover, so those two are described from secondary
characterisation and should be treated as unverified.*

## Never

Observed absences, counted rather than assumed.

- **Never "I"** in the absent-witness register. Zero, in 13,500 words.
- **Never exclaim.** One exclamation mark across three full classic episodes.
- **Never "Yet" as an opener.** Effectively zero in the corpus, and the first word pastiche reaches
  for.
- **Never ask rhetorical questions in bulk.** Zero to five question marks per episode, and most are
  genuine setups answered by the next shot.
- **Never use the past tense for what is happening now.**
- **Never restate the image.**
- **Never use an intensifier where a number belongs.**
- **Never name the narrator's feelings.** Awe is produced by the fact, not asserted about the
  speaker.

## How pastiche fails

Ranked by how fast each gives the game away.

**"Here we see" in the first sentence.** The corpus opens with a noun phrase, a place or a fact.
"Here" opens under one percent of real sentences and nearly every imitation, because pastiche
captions the image and the register never does.

**No numbers.** The single fastest tell. Substituting superlatives for measurement produces a
trailer, not a documentary.

**Deliberate long-short alternation.** Covered above. It is the most common piece of bad advice
about this register and the corpus does the opposite.

**Unrelenting irony.** The classic move is narrating a mundane human scene as wildlife. Funny once.
The register itself contains no irony at all, so once the joke lands there is nothing left and the
third sentence has to escalate. Escalation is what turns it into parody: real narration does not
escalate, it accumulates.

**Mixing the three registers.** Absent-witness prose with participant punctuation, or Herzog's "I"
with Nat Geo jeopardy.

**Anthropomorphising with intent rather than with grammar.** Real narration does attribute purpose,
in flat declaratives that stay inside the animal's actual behaviour. Pastiche gives animals jobs and
opinions, then narrates the joke instead of the animal.

**No silence.** Narration is written against picture and leaves long unnarrated stretches; the words
carry perhaps a third of an episode. Pastiche is continuous prose, so it has the density of an essay
and none of the pacing, which is why it exhausts a reader by the third sentence even when every
sentence is well formed.
