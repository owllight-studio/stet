# The Kitchen

Counted off the essay that made his name and the narration that made him famous. Two registers, and
they are measurably different.

## The one rule

**Implicate yourself before you implicate anybody else.**

Everything that makes this voice bearable rather than insufferable runs through that. The contempt
is real, but it is aimed at himself first, and after that upward at people with more power than he
had. It is never aimed down.

## The number that ends the argument about profanity

**One f-word in 2,573 words.** That is 0.39 per thousand, in the piece that built his entire
reputation for swearing, and it sits inside a quotation of kitchen Spanish that he then
mock-translates into elaborate courtesy.

Profanity here is a punchline or a piece of reported speech. It is essentially never an intensifier.
The test: delete it and see whether anything is lost. In his work something is. In imitation nothing
is.

**The mean drops 40 percent and the long sentence effectively disappears.** But the variance holds,
so he did not flatten out. He kept the rhythm and cut the ceiling.

Two things worth reading off that table. **Second person is constant across both**, which means
"you" is not a book device or a television device, it is simply how he addresses anybody. And
**first person drops by a third** in the narration: on the page he is the subject, on screen the
place is.

About 36 percent of voiceover sentences carry no auxiliary or copula at all and run under 12 words.
Fragments are the base unit there, not an occasional effect.

## Rules

### The list accelerates, then turns

Concrete nouns gathering speed, and the last item changes register or admits something.

> "I wanted it all: the cuts and burns on hands and wrists, the ghoulish kitchen humor, the free
> food, the pilfered booze, the camaraderie that flourished within rigid order and nerve-shattering
> chaos."

Physical, physical, physical, petty, and then the last item is the real answer and is abstract. Note
that the order is a hierarchy someone assembled from memory rather than a set someone researched.

### Put the reader in the room. Do not order them around

Measured: **two sentence-initial imperatives in the entire essay**, against second person appearing
in 20 percent of sentences. Imitators have this exactly backwards and write nothing but commands.

**Yes:** "Say it's a quiet Monday night, and you've just checked your coat in that swanky Art Deco
update in the Flatiron district..."
**No:** a string of instructions about what you should order.

The command form does exist and it is always a dare rather than a direction: "If you like
four-day-old fish, be my guest."

### Name the specific thing

> "But just about the only place you'll see anyone in the kitchen wearing a hat or a hairnet is
> Blimpie."

Not "fast food chains." *Blimpie.* The proper noun does the work an adjective would do badly, and it
is proof of having been there. Same mechanism in the narration: barberries, chelo-kabob, D.J. Kool
Herc.

### Contempt is the setup. The reversal is the payload

Pile up the hostile adjectives and let them collapse inside the same sentence.

> "For a moment, or a second, the pinched expressions of the cynical, world-weary, throat-cutting,
> miserable bastards we've all had to become disappears, when we're confronted with something as
> simple as a plate of food."

The essay does the comic version too: obscene kitchen Spanish, then a straight-faced translation
into "Please reduce some additional demi-glace, brother."

### The self-implicating aside goes in a subordinate clause

So it does not read as a bid for credit. "Fresh from that experience, I began thinking about
becoming a traitor to my profession."

And when it is direct, it costs him something: **"There was a period in my life in the kitchen where
I was an asshole. I was."**

### Present tense, even for things long over

Six to one in the essay. Past tense arrives only inside an anecdote.

### One comic simile from far outside food, never clustered

"Line cooking, done well, is a dance, a high-speed Balanchine collaboration." "Being a chef is a lot
like being an air-traffic controller."

## Never

- **Never swear for texture.** 0.39 per thousand words. It is a punchline or a quotation.
- **Never use food-writing vocabulary.** Thirty stock terms were tested against both corpora:
  unctuous, silky, toothsome, succulent, velvety, decadent, sublime, mouthfeel, drizzle, artisanal,
  umami, explosion of flavour. **Total hits: one "infused" in the essay, three in 30,000 words of
  transcript.** He describes food by naming its parts, never its effect on him.
- **Never lean on intensifiers.** In the essay: "very" once, and "really", "amazing", "incredible"
  and "literally" zero times each.
- **Never run two long sentences together.** No two sentences of 35-plus words are adjacent anywhere
  in the essay. The long one is always paid off by a short one.
- **Never let an insult be the ending.**
- **Never withhold the specific noun.** Blimpie, not "a chain". Balanchine, not "a dancer".
- **Never claim expertise you lack.** "I'm not a cook, nor am I a journalist." The disclaimer is
  habitual.
- **Never write self-implication that flatters you.**

One note on punctuation. The essay runs em dashes at 12.4 per thousand words and semicolons at 3.0,
which tells you the appositive hinge is doing the work and the balance a semicolon implies is not
wanted. **In a project that bans em dashes, that hinge becomes a full stop and a fragment**, which
is what the voiceover does anyway. Do not substitute a semicolon; it is the wrong instrument.

## What he took back, and what he did not

This matters for anyone borrowing the voice, because he audited it himself.

After Weinstein and Batali he repudiated what *Kitchen Confidential* had celebrated, without
hedging: "To what extent in that book did I provide validation to meatheads?" And: "I found myself
in this very old, very, frankly, phallocentric, very oppressive system and I was proud of myself for
surviving it. And I celebrated that rather enthusiastically."

Asked whether the book's claim that kitchens were too busy to care about gender or race was naive,
his entire answer was: **"Yes. I just have to cop to that and say yes."**

He also disowned the persona, in 2010: "I'm not the bad-boy chef... I'm not a chef. I'm not bad. And
I'm not a boy."

**What he did not retract: the prose, the profanity, or the affection for cooks.** He retracted the
aspirational framing. The style survived his own repudiation of its content, and that is precisely
the distinction imitation misses.

On what writing about other people's food owes them: "People are telling you a story when they give
you food. And if you don't accept the food, you are in many cultures rejecting the people." And on
his own position: "It's a gloriously doomed enterprise. I'm in the business of finding great places,
and then we fuck them up."

**Two attributions to get right.** There is no sourceable Bourdain statement drawing a
traveller-versus-tourist binary; the nearest real thing is a preference for arriving without an
itinerary. And the widely quoted "travel changes you, leaves marks on you" passage is filed under
*The Nasty Bits*, not *No Reservations*, with no page citation. Do not ship either unchecked.

## How pastiche fails

**Profanity as personality.** Measured at 0.39 per thousand words in the source and run at several
per paragraph by imitators. In Bourdain it carries information: the register of the person quoted,
or the exact size of his own contempt. In pastiche it carries only the writer's wish to seem
unimpressed, and you can delete it without losing anything.

**Contempt aimed downward.** His goes at himself first, then at owners, consultants and celebrity
chefs, upward at people with more power than he had. Pastiche aims it at tourists, at diners, at
people who like the wrong things, because that is easier and costs nothing. The structural tell is
the reversal running backwards: no drop into tenderness, so the paragraph ends where his would have
turned.

**Researched detail instead of remembered detail.** His proper nouns are load-bearing because he was
there. The imitator knows the shape of the move and fills it with names looked up, which produces
detail that is accurate but not *selective*. The tell is that every detail is the same type and the
same importance.

**Swearing plus "unctuous" in the same sentence.** The cliché avoidance is silent, so it does not
get copied, while the swearing is loud, so it does. That combination appears nowhere in his work.

**Fragments end to end.** The most-copied feature and the worst-copied. His fragments sit inside a
distribution with an sd around 0.6 to 0.7 of the mean and 30 percent of sentences in the 10-to-19
word band. **The rhythm is a contrast effect and it requires the long sentences the imitator threw
away.**

**Twenty-eight years.** He worked kitchens from roughly 1978 to the late nineties before the essay,
which is why the self-implication reads as fact rather than modesty, and why he could retract the
book's ethics in 2017 without the prose collapsing: the authority was in the having-been-there, not
in the attitude. An imitator has only the attitude, so the self-deprecation reads as a move.

**Detection:** count the swearing per thousand words and check whether each instance is quoted
speech or a punchline. Then find the self-implicating sentence and ask whether it costs the writer
anything. Nobody imitating him writes a sentence that does.
