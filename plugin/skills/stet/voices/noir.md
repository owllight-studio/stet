# Noir

Counted from the texts: 5,263 sentences of *The Big Sleep*, 2,723 narration sentences of
*Red Harvest*, with simile density computed rather than estimated.

## The one rule

**The simile is rare.** One per 545 words in Chandler, one per 1,969 in Hammett. Once every two
pages, and once every seven. The most imitated feature of this register is, inside the register, its
scarcest move, and pastiche runs it twenty to forty times too often.

A figure is the payoff to a run of flat sentences. Remove the flat run and there is no debt to
discharge, so the figures queue up and each devalues the last.

## The feeling, and how it gets there

**Dread, and a grief the narrator will not admit to.**

The register is often mistaken for coldness. It is the opposite: it is a man feeling far too much
and refusing to say so, and every rule here exists to get that onto the page without him saying it.

**"Never name an emotion at the moment it is felt" is not a ban on emotion.** It is the delivery
mechanism. "I was frightened" tells you nothing. *"I couldn't hear my own footsteps. It was the walk
of a dead man"* puts you inside the fear, and it does it by describing a body rather than a mood.

The simile is where the judgment lives, because the flat declarative is forbidden from
editorialising. "The sunshine was as empty as a headwaiter's smile" is not about sunshine. It is
about paid professional insincerity, which is the book's actual subject, and the whole opinion
travels inside the figure.

And the narrator loses. Every canonical one is dead, bleeding out, or implicated. That is the
emotional load the deadpan is holding up, and without it the diction has nothing to defend against
and reads as posturing.

## Rules

### Flat declaratives, and let the range be wide

Subject, verb, object, stop. Half of Chandler's sentences are ten words or fewer. A fifth are five
or fewer.

But the load-bearing fact is the **range**, not the mean: from 4 words to 97 in the same book.
Pastiche clusters in a 6-to-12 band and never writes a long one, which makes it metrically flat.
Uniform staccato is as monotonous as uniform ornament, only louder.

**Yes:** "The city wasn't pretty." (Hammett, *Red Harvest*)

### Past tense, first person, and the narrator already knows

Without exception across every exemplar. The retrospection is the engine. Two of the films make it
physical: a man bleeding into a dictaphone, a corpse in a swimming pool.

### Never open on a subordinate clause

Under two percent of narration sentences in both measured texts. No "Although", no "Having", no
"Because", no "As I". Parataxis: independent clauses in sequence, joined by "and" or by a full stop.

Nearly a quarter of Chandler's narration sentences open with a bare "I".

### Feeling arrives as a physical fact

Never name an emotion at the moment it is felt.

**Yes:** "I couldn't hear my own footsteps. It was the walk of a dead man." (*Double Indemnity*)
**No:** "I was frightened."

### Weather sets a clock, not a mood

Given as fact, early, and deliberately as a non-event. *The Big Sleep* opens on weather that has not
happened yet. It is never pathetic fallacy and it is never asked to mean anything.

### Rooms are inventoried, not evoked

Objects named in the order the eye hits them, with their prices and their pretensions noted. A
room's job is to expose whoever chose it.

### The narrator loses

Every canonical one is dead, bleeding out, implicated, or about to be. Toughness is legible only
against actual loss. Give the narrator competence and control and the hard-boiled diction has
nothing to defend against, which reads as posturing. This failure is invisible at sentence level and
total at page level.

**Yes:** "Me, I was part of the nastiness now." (Chandler, *The Big Sleep*)

## The simile

The register's signature, and the thing that has to be got exactly right because everything else
survives being got slightly wrong.

**The vehicle is more concrete and more social than the tenor.** "The sunshine was as empty as a
headwaiter's smile." Empty sunshine is unpicturable; a headwaiter's smile is a specific human face.
The comparison converts an abstraction into a person. Reverse it and it fails instantly.

**The vehicle comes from the narrator's own world.** Chandler's are drawn from service work,
funerals, restaurants, kitchens, boxing: undertakers, aldermen, a bishop, a bride's pie crust. They
characterise the man speaking as much as the thing described. A vehicle he would have no reason to
know breaks the voice, and the voice is the only thing holding the prose together.

**It is where the judgment hides.** The flat declarative is forbidden from editorialising, so
opinion travels inside the figures. That headwaiter line is not about sunshine, it is about paid
professional insincerity, which is the book's actual subject. Every good one carries a verdict or a
threat. None merely decorates.

**It stops.** Two to five words for the vehicle, no second clause. "as limp as a fresh-killed
rabbit" does not go on to tell you about the rabbit. Six to thirteen words for the whole sentence.

**It sits after flatness, never after another figure.** No two consecutive similes appear anywhere
in the sampled narration.

### How a simile fails

- The vehicle is more exotic than the tenor, so the reader now has two problems instead of one.
- Both terms are abstract. No image forms, and the sentence produces a feeling of wit with nothing
  attached. The most common failure.
- The vehicle keeps talking. A second beat turns the figure into a bit and moves the reader's
  attention from the scene to the author.
- It flatters the narrator. Real ones convict the world or the speaker.
- It arrives with company. The second one is read as a style, and once the reader is reading a style
  the register is dead.
- It has no verdict. Surprise alone is a crossword clue.

## Never

- Never present tense.
- Never name an emotion as it happens.
- Never explain the joke. The deadpan line is followed by the next piece of business.
- Never use an exclamation mark in narration. One appears in all of *The Big Sleep*.
- Never stack adjectives. Hammett introduces Spade in nouns and geometry and spends one adjective
  phrase at the end.
- Never let the narrator state his own decency. Chandler's own rule: honour "by instinct, by
  inevitability, without thought of it, and certainly without saying it."
- Never let the narrator come out clean.
- Never use the genre's furniture as scenery. The fedora, the neon and the venetian blinds belong to
  the adaptations. The prose names dairy trucks, a stamp collection, Twin Oaks Tavern.

## How pastiche fails

**Simile inflation, twenty to forty times over.** Past about one per paragraph the reader stops
tracking the scene and starts scanning for the next figure. Three consecutive figures is exactly
where the pattern becomes visible, which is why three sentences is the tolerance limit.

**No baseline, so no rhythm.** Rhythm here is the contrast between plain and figured. A text made
only of highlights has none.

**Vehicle drift.** Comparison objects picked for surprise alone come from nowhere in particular, so
they stop characterising the narrator and start characterising the writer's search for something
odd.

**Mood substituted for event.** The originals are plot machines running at speed. Cain puts a broke
man on a road with a decision in nine words. Pastiche writes weather, cigarettes and rumination with
nobody wanting anything. Terseness exists to compress incident; with no incident it is just
withholding.

**Costume nouns instead of world nouns.** Naming the genre's furniture announces that the writer is
doing a voice rather than reporting a place.

**Deadpan with no straight man.** Keillor's parody survives because he puts the voice into
Minnesota, a world that flatly declines to be noir. Most pastiche builds a world that agrees with
the voice, which removes the resistance the deadpan plays against.

### The documented case

*Blade Runner*'s theatrical narration, written by an uncredited hand after several rejected drafts,
recorded over its lead's objection, and removed entirely in the Director's Cut.

The mechanical diagnosis: this register is a **compression** device, and it was laid over material
that was already slow, already atmospheric, and already legible on screen. So the narration told the
audience what they were currently looking at. Its most-mocked line fails every simile test at once:
the vehicle is exotic, the joke is explained inside the same breath, and it flatters the speaker
without convicting anybody.
