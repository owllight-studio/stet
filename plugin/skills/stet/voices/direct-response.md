# Direct Response

Counted off the artifacts rather than the folklore. *Scientific Advertising* is 1,854 sentences at a
mean of 11.7 words. Halbert's 1986 headline newsletter runs a median of 6, with 46 percent of
sentences at five words or fewer. Ogilvy's Rolls-Royce body sits at a median of 13.

The register did not get uniformly shorter across sixty years. **It got more variable.** Hopkins is
evenly clipped, with only 11 percent of sentences at five words or fewer. Halbert alternates a
forty-word run with a one-word paragraph, and 38 to 46 percent of his sentences are five words or
fewer. The fragment rate is the signature, not the mean.

And "write in second person" is wrong as stated. Measured you-and-your density per thousand words:
Ogilvy's Rolls 7.4, Hopkins 13.7, Halbert 21.7 to 24.5. Ogilvy uses "I" zero times in the Rolls ad;
Halbert uses it 14 to 28 times per thousand words. There are two branches. The product-argument
branch is third person about the product and second person about the reader's use of it. The letter
branch is a first-person voice talking to one reader.

## The one rule

**Every claim carries a number somebody could go and check.**

Hopkins' reason is still the whole argument: a general claim gets discounted, and a specific claim is
either true or a lie. Precision is not a style choice. It is the reader's only way to tell which one
they are reading.

"Prices reduced" becomes "reduced 25 percent". A razor becomes "a 78-second shave". Ogilvy's Rolls
ad carries 14 distinct quantities in 13 points: seven hours at full throttle, five coats of primer,
nine of finish, eight hides of leather. **Each number is standing where an adjective was deleted.**

The condition attached, and it is new: precision alone no longer buys credibility. Precise numbers
raise perceived competence, and sceptical readers resist the effect (Xie and Kronrod, 2012). The
number has to survive a search, or it is discounted exactly like the adjective it replaced.

### Then restate it as something a person can picture

The move most people miss, and Ogilvy does it in the same breath as the number. Eight hides of
leather means nothing. **"Enough to make 128 pairs of soft shoes"** is a picture.

The number establishes that you counted. The restatement is what the reader keeps.

## The feeling, and how it gets there

**Trust, which is the only feeling this register is trying to produce.**

Everything mechanical here serves one emotional end: the reader deciding you are not lying to them.

That is why the specific number beats the adjective, why the proof lands before the claim, and why
volunteering a real flaw earns more than any superlative. Ogilvy quotes a motoring magazine's
engineer rather than asserting his own car is quiet, and Sugarman spends the first third of an ad
not believing in the product.

The register also has the sharpest example anywhere of feeling done wrong. Manufactured urgency and
simulated intimacy are attempts to produce an emotion the situation does not contain, and they are
now measurably counterproductive. The reader feels the machinery instead of the offer.

## Rules

### Proof arrives before the claim

The most consistently violated structural rule in the register, and the one that does the most work.

Ogilvy attributes his own headline to the Technical Editor of *The Motor* rather than asserting it.
Sugarman spends the first third of a sunglasses ad being unconvinced by sunglasses. The order is
borrowed authority, then personal experience, then mechanism, then guarantee.

**Yes:** open unconvinced and convert on the page.
**No:** open at maximum volume and never supply proof.

### Select the reader, do not attract them

Hopkins on headlines: *"The purpose of a headline is to pick out people you can interest."* Not to be
clever, not to be liked. A headline that interests everybody has selected nobody.

Caples' four openings, strongest first: self-interest, news, curiosity, a quick easy way. Curiosity
is the weakest and the riskiest.

A headline that repels the wrong reader is doing its job. That framing has aged far better than the
attention-grabbing one that replaced it.

**Use odd numbers.** Halbert dissects his own sample headline word by word and lands on this: "the
specificity of 17-Days makes our headline more believable." Round numbers read as estimates. Odd
ones read as measurements, because a measurement is what they usually are.

### Find out what the reader already knows before deciding what to say

Schwartz's diagnostic, and it is the one part of this register that is genuinely a system.

Desire is a fixed quantity already present in the market. *"Copy cannot create desire for a
product."* So the writer's job is to locate the reader, not to generate wanting.

**Awareness** decides what the headline is allowed to mention: the offer, the claim, the desire, the
problem, or just the reader's own identity, in that descending order of knowledge. Address someone
unaware of the problem with the offer and the sentence is unreadable to them.

**Sophistication** decides how hard the headline has to work. Once every version of a promise has
been heard, the only fresh thing left to say is *how*, and the mechanism moves into the headline.

### Every joint gets a short sentence

The "slippery slide" is a chain of single purposes: each element exists only to get the next one
read. Mechanically it is rhythm, not mood.

In Sugarman's 1987 BluBlocker ad, the first sentence is nine words, declarative, and names no
product, no benefit and no adjective. At every point a reader could stop, a three-word sentence sits
instead: "Let me explain." "I found out." "It was obvious." Those alternate with explanatory
sentences of 20 to 30 words.

The widely repeated one-word-paragraph claim does not hold. The shortest units in that ad are three
words.

### Volunteer the flaw, and only if it is real

The best-evidenced technique here. Two-sided advertising has a curvilinear benefit, and
**voluntariness is what makes it work**: a caveat you offer earns credibility, a caveat you were
forced into does not (Eisend, 2006).

Which means it stops working the moment it is a move. A flaw invented to look candid is another
manipulation, and readers who have seen the pattern read it that way.

### The ask is an instruction plus the apparatus

Never implied, never one word. Ogilvy's price sits in the second-to-last paragraph, and the final
paragraph names the action, the dealer list, the address and the phone number. Sugarman closes with
a test the reader performs at home, then a coupon with part numbers, postage and a toll-free line.

### Plain language, and this one has a number on it

Needless complexity lowers judged author intelligence independent of content quality (Oppenheimer,
2006). Unbounce's 2024 benchmark across more than 41,000 landing pages: pages at fifth to seventh
grade reading level converted at 11.1 percent against 5.3 percent for professional-level prose.

Hopkins got there first and put it harder: *"Platitudes and generalities roll off the human
understanding like water from a duck."*

## Never

- **Never fabricate a testimonial, review, customer or screenshot of one.** This stopped being a
  taste question on 21 October 2024. 16 CFR Part 465 makes it a deceptive practice to write, sell or
  disseminate a testimonial misrepresenting that the reviewer exists, that they used the product, or
  what happened. §465.4 also bans paying for a review conditioned on its sentiment. Civil penalties
  attach.
- **Never manufacture urgency or scarcity.** No resetting countdown, no invented stock level, no
  closing-tonight that reopens tomorrow. The FTC names False Low Stock, False Activity, False High
  Demand and Baseless Countdown Timer as distinct dark patterns. And it does not work: tested in
  isolation, scarcity cues and countdown timers produced no significant lift, 14.3 percent against
  11.3 percent control, while aggressive dark patterns produced 65 dropouts against 9 and measurably
  more frustration (Luguri and Strahilevitz, 2021, n=3,777). Sites without scarcity cues rate more
  trustworthy. Note that this one is in the canon and not only in the imitators: Caples closes with
  "before it's too late to gain the special benefits of this offer" and no deadline is ever stated.
  There is nothing to be too late for. That is the most-copied line shape in the register and it is
  a lie by implication.
- **Never use the preemptive claim.** Hopkins' Schlitz method describes an industry-standard process
  as though it were exclusive, and he admits it: "All claims were such as any brewer might have
  made." It is a lie by implication even when every sentence is true, and it does not survive a
  reader who can search.
- **Never simulate intimacy.** Merged first names in a headline, fake handwriting, the spontaneous
  postscript, the I-am-writing-this-at-2am aside. Halbert's best attention device was printing the
  recipient's name in the headline of a mailed letter, which is manufactured intimacy at industrial
  scale. The machinery is visible to everyone now.
- **Never use an unfalsifiable superlative.** Hopkins: they cause readers "to discount the statements
  you make from that point on."
- **Never entertain.** Hopkins twice: "No one reads ads for amusement", and "Ads are not written to
  entertain." Fine writing "reveals the hook".
- **Never let the selling show.** "Any studied attempt to sell, if apparent, creates corresponding
  resistance."
- **Never shout.** Hopkins objects to oversized type on the grounds that nobody admires a salesman
  who talks in a loud voice.
- **Never pad to length.** "Long copy outsells short copy" is folklore with no dataset under it. The
  classics earned their length by having that much information.
- **Never give a number without saying what it is a number of.**

## How pastiche fails

Imitation copies the tokens and skips the substrate. Short paragraphs, "But there's more", caps
subheads, yellow highlight, "Dear Friend", the P.S. What it does not copy is what those devices were
built to carry: one checkable specific, a mechanism explaining why the claim is true, and a guarantee
somebody honours. **The form was a delivery system for information density. Imitating the form
without the information produces a shape with nothing in it.**

**Specificity inverts into invented precision.** "78-second shave" and "eight hides of English
leather" point at something verifiable. "This weird 7-second morning ritual" uses identical syntax
and points at nothing. Same grammar, opposite epistemics, and readers now sort them by whether the
number survives a search.

**The proof order reverses**, which is why pastiche feels like being shouted at by a stranger.

**The shape itself is now a signal.** Unfalsifiable claim, plus urgency, plus testimonial, minus
mechanism, is the structural fingerprint of a scam. Honest copy adopting that structure inherits the
suspicion. This is persuasion knowledge operating at the level of genre rather than sentence
(Friestad and Wright, 1994): once a reader recognises the *format*, they stop evaluating the offer
and start evaluating the offerer.

**The personality is templated.** Halbert works partly because there is a real hill he jogged, a
real boat he would rather be on, and a real opinion about a televangelist. He opens one issue with
"My head hurts, my feet stink and I don't love Jimmy Swaggert", and follows it with "Writing sucks.
I hate to write." That is a specific person, and you cannot reach it by swapping nouns in a story
template.

**Hopkins diagnosed the mechanism in 1923 and it is still the best available.** He warns against
fine writing and unique literary style on one ground: *"They reveal the hook."* Any technique that
becomes visible as technique converts into resistance.

In 1923 the visible technique was ornate prose. **In 2026 it is the direct-response cadence
itself.** The staccato fragment, the ellipsis, the unearned "But wait." have been used so heavily by
product launches and dropship funnels that the rhythm now functions as a genre marker for a scam. A
reader who sees three one-word paragraphs has classified the page before reading a single claim.

## The load-bearing irony

Hopkins, Ogilvy, Caples and Halbert were each writing *against* the dominant sales register of their
moment. Hopkins wrote plainly when advertising was florid. Ogilvy wrote 719 factual words when car
ads were photographs of women on bonnets. Halbert wrote like a man talking when direct mail was
formal.

**So imitating their output reproduces the opposite of their method**, because the method was to
sound unlike the prevailing sales voice, and their output is now the prevailing sales voice.

The escape is to keep the substrate and drop the costume. Keep the falsifiable claim, the named
source, the plain syntax, the short paragraph, the one concrete ask. Drop the cadence tics, the
manufactured urgency, the simulated intimacy, and the narrator who never existed.

That happens to be the only version a corpus-backed site can support anyway, because the substrate
is the part you cannot fake and it is the part you already have.

And there is one thing pastiche never copies, because it is invisible on the page. **Every writer in
this file could measure whether it worked.** Hopkins keyed his coupons. Caples' ad was tracked by
returns in *Physical Culture*. Halbert reports re-heading an ad and lifting response 475 percent.
The specificity in the copy is downstream of a measurement discipline behind the copy.

Imitating the prose without the measurement produces the tone of certainty with none of the basis
for it, and readers detect that gap even when they cannot name it.

One correction while we are here, because the number gets repeated everywhere: the claim that the
Rolls-Royce ad lifted sales 50 percent is not supported by Ogilvy's own account, which gives no
figure at all.

One note on the legal floor, because the easy narrative gets it backwards. The FTC's 2024
click-to-cancel rule was vacated on procedural grounds, and Part 425 formally reverted on 12
February 2026, removing "Simple cancellation" from the CFR. The floor under subscription-retention
dark patterns got *lower* this year, not higher. That is an argument for holding a standard above
the floor, not evidence that the practice became acceptable.

## The finding that matters beyond this file

Altay and Gilardi, *PNAS Nexus*, 2024. Preregistered, n=4,976. **Labelling a headline as
AI-generated lowered its perceived accuracy and readers' willingness to share it, regardless of
whether the headline was true and regardless of who actually wrote it.**

The penalty attaches to perceived authorship, not to authorship. So copy that merely *reads* as
machine-generated plausibly pays that penalty with no label attached and no way to appeal it.

For a register whose entire mechanism is credibility, that is not a style problem. It is the
conversion rate.
